# It's not the washing machine you picked back up.

## But the role that comes with it.

---

You asked them to start a load of laundry. They said yes. An hour later, the machine hasn't moved. You remind them. They walk over, open the door, and ask which program. You explain. They hesitate. And at some point, you take it back yourself. It's simpler that way.

But is it really simpler? Or is it just faster — and a lot heavier?

---

What just happened isn't about laundry. It's about roles.

They played the incompetent one. Not necessarily on purpose. Maybe they really don't know which program. But that incompetence has a function: it exonerates them. It protects them from making a mistake, from being evaluated, from not doing it the right way. In Transactional Analysis, this is called the Wooden Leg. _I'm willing, but I don't know how._ And as long as they don't know, they don't have to try.

You played the Rescuer. The one who knows, who does, who carries. And that role confirms something you've believed for a long time: that if you don't do it, it won't get done. Or it won't be done right.

These two roles fit together perfectly. They feed each other. And they can keep turning like this for years.

---

When you pick the laundry back up, you pick up everything else too. The mental list of what's missing. The appointments not to forget. Everything that holds things together without anyone noticing. You didn't decide this all at once — it settled in gradually, in small steps, each one reasonable on its own. _It's faster if I do it. They don't know where things are kept._

And you carry. And you keep carrying. And somewhere, you're waiting for the other person to see it.

But here's what nobody says out loud: have you actually told them what you were carrying? Not the evening's reproach. Not the sigh when picking things back up. The real conversation — the one where you say _I'm exhausted from holding everything together, and I need you to take your share_?

Because the other person doesn't see the list. They see someone who manages well. Who knows what they're doing. Who picks things back up because they prefer it that way. So they pull back a little more — which confirms to you that you need to do even more.

Two people drifting apart. Each convinced it's the other one's fault. And neither one has actually put their cards on the table.

---

In the evening, something gives.

The reproach comes out — sometimes out loud, sometimes in a silence that says the same thing without words. _You didn't do anything. As usual._ And there, the exhausted Rescuer becomes the Persecutor.

From the outside, it looks like a disproportionate attack over a washing machine. They end up as the Victim — accused of an incompetence they were never really given the chance to move past. The Karpman Triangle has turned. Nobody won.

But ask yourself this: is the evening's reproach really about the laundry? Or is it about everything that hasn't been said for months?

---

What makes this scene hard to see is that it looks perfectly reasonable from the outside.

There's a real question of how tasks get divided. There's real exhaustion. All of that is true. But behind what's real, there's the game. And the game has its own hidden benefits that neither person wants to look at directly.

The Rescuer who does everything is always right. They're indispensable. They're in control. But have you ever considered that this control costs you more than it protects you?

The Wooden Leg who doesn't know is protected. They don't have to perform, don't have to risk getting it wrong. But after years of never trying, they end up feeling incompetent in their own home. Diminished. And they don't say that either.

The real question — the one nobody asks: who benefits from nothing changing?

---

As long as you're looking at the surface — the machine, the program, the evening's reproach — you stay in the argument. You negotiate tasks. You put systems in place. And six months later, the scene starts again with a different backdrop.

Seeing the game means asking yourself what this role gives you that you're not willing to ask for any other way. What it costs you that you'd rather not count.

The real question isn't how to divide the chores more fairly. It's: what have you been pretending not to see all along?
