# You No Longer Speak the Same Language — and It's Not a Communication Problem

_Series "Growing Up — Without Losing Each Other" · Article 3 · Subscribers Only_

---

We were coming back from that dinner. In the car, I had started talking about what I'd observed at the table — that friend who kept interrupting his wife, the poorly expressed need behind the gesture, the relational dynamic replaying on a loop without anyone noticing.

She had responded. A real response, not polite acquiescence. She said that this kind of situation exhausted her — these dinners where women disappear from the conversation while men take up all the space. That it was always the same. That nobody seemed to find it problematic.

I had wanted to continue. To talk about what I saw beneath the surface — the needs, the mechanisms, the way both participants together maintained a dynamic that neither of them had really chosen. She looked out the window. "Yeah. Maybe."

The conversation stopped there.

For a long time, I believed it was a communication problem. That I hadn't found the right words. That I could have explained better, introduced the subject better, created more space for her to enter into what I was trying to say.

What I understood later is that the problem wasn't in the words. It was beneath the words. We weren't looking at the same thing — not because one of the two readings was wrong, but because they came from different values, different questions, a different way of organizing what matters.

It wasn't a communication problem. It was a divergence of worldview.

---

## What We Call a "Communication Problem" When It Isn't One

There's a widespread belief in personal development and couples coaching: most conflicts come from poor communication. If we learn to listen better, to paraphrase, to express our needs without attacking — disagreements get resolved.

This belief is partially true. For a large category of tensions, communication tools make a real difference. When two people fundamentally want the same thing but struggle to express it — nonviolent communication, active listening, and mirroring have an effect.

But there's another category of disagreements. Those where both people are communicating correctly, genuinely listening to each other, not attacking — and still failing to understand one another. Not because something is going wrong in the exchange. But because what they're trying to say comes from fundamentally different places.

In those situations, improving communication resolves nothing. You become more skilled at telling each other you disagree — but you don't get any closer.

---

## Two Ways of Organizing the World

What distinguishes these two categories is what might be called the level of values organization — the way a person structures what matters, what is real, what deserves attention.

It's not a question of intelligence. It's not a question of good or bad intentions. It's a way of inhabiting the world that is built over time, through experiences, crises, and moments of questioning. And it can evolve — but at its own pace, not because someone else has decided the time has come.

In a couple where one person has gone through deep personal development work and the other hasn't — or in a couple where both have grown but in different directions — this divergence creates something specific. Not incompatibility. An asymmetry in the way situations are read, questions are posed, and decisions are made about what deserves attention.

Concretely, it looks like this.

One arrives in a situation and asks: what is actually going on here? What needs are at work? What underlying dynamic is producing what I see on the surface? They look for the invisible structure behind the visible. They're interested in root causes, patterns, what connects events to one another.

The other arrives in the same situation and asks: what is happening here? Who is affected? What is just or unjust in what I see? They seek to name what is present — the concrete effects, the power dynamics, what is actually touching people. They're interested in what is visible, tangible, immediately felt.

These two ways of looking are each coherent within their own logic. They don't contradict each other in the strict sense — they simply aren't looking at the same place. And that is precisely what makes conversations between them go in circles. Each responds to the question they are asking themselves, not the one the other is asking. And nobody realizes these are two different questions.

## Why Spiral Dynamics Illuminates What Nonviolent Communication Cannot Resolve

There is a model that maps these different ways of organizing the world — Spiral Dynamics, developed by Clare Graves and popularized notably by Don Beck and Ken Wilber. I won't give an exhaustive presentation of it here. What interests me in this context is what it makes visible that communication tools alone cannot.

Spiral Dynamics proposes that individuals — and couples, groups, cultures — move through successive stages of values organization. Each stage responds to particular life conditions, poses particular questions, and values particular things. And each stage is a coherent response to those conditions — not a "better" or "worse" level than the others.

What is useful here is the description of what happens when two people find themselves at different stages — or in transition between different stages.

A person firmly anchored in a logic of performance and concrete results seeks efficiency, clarity, and practical solutions in their relationships. They evaluate situations according to what they concretely produce. Conversations about "underlying dynamics" or "deep needs" seem abstract to them, even a way of avoiding what really matters.

A person transitioning toward a more relational and systemic logic seeks depth, connection, and mutual understanding in their relationships. They evaluate situations according to what they mean for the people involved. Conversations about concrete results seem to miss what truly counts.

And a person who has integrated a more integrative logic seeks to hold both. They see the value of efficiency and the value of depth. They can navigate between levels. But that very capacity creates its own problem: they can seem elusive to someone still solidly anchored in a single level. You don't know where they stand; you don't know on which terrain they'll respond.

In a couple, this divergence isn't always clearly visible. It shows up in the subjects you avoid. In conversations that go in circles. In that diffuse feeling that the other is responding beside the point — not because they aren't listening, but because they're answering a different question from the one you were asking.

## What You Cannot Do — and Why That Matters

Before talking about possible bridges, something needs to be named clearly: you cannot make the other person change their stage of development. Not through pedagogy, not through example, not through patience, not through gentle pressure. Transitions from one level to another happen when internal and external conditions are aligned — and at a pace that belongs only to the person going through them.

This isn't fatalism. It's a clarification that helps avoid two symmetrical pitfalls.

The first pitfall: believing that with enough of the right words, enough well-explained frameworks, enough patience — the other person will eventually see what you see. This belief produces Saviors, Normative Parents, exhaustion. And often disappointment.

The second pitfall: concluding that the distance is insurmountable and the relationship is doomed. This conclusion moves too fast. A divergence of worldview does not make a relationship impossible — it makes it different from what you imagined. And different is not the same as impossible.

What you can act on is the quality of connection within the distance. Not erasing the difference — but creating spaces where it isn't the main obstacle.

## Three Ways to Build a Bridge Without Denying the Distance

### Bridge 1 — Find the Real Common Ground, Not the Ground You Wish You Had

In any divergence of worldview, there are overlapping zones — values, concerns, ways of seeing that converge despite everything. The problem is that we tend to look for this common ground in the wrong place — in the subjects that are contested, in the conversations that have failed.

The real common ground is often found elsewhere. In shared experiences that predate the divergence. In values that express themselves differently but come from the same place. In the concrete concerns of daily life — the couple, the children, the home, the projects — where both people fundamentally want the same thing, even if they don't frame it the same way.

Practically: keep a mental map of the subjects and contexts where exchanges flow naturally, where there is genuine connection. Don't treat these as exceptions to the rule of distance — treat them as a map of the existing common ground. And invest those spaces, consciously, without waiting for the areas of divergence to resolve themselves.

### Bridge 2 — Translate, Don't Convince

When a divergence of worldview is at stake, the temptation is to convince — to explain your way of seeing, to demonstrate its coherence, to bring the other person to understand it. This temptation is natural. It rarely produces what you hope for.

Translating is different. It means starting from where the other person is — from their questions, their values, what matters to them — and finding how what you want to say connects with that. Not disguising your thinking to make it palatable. Finding the real point of contact between two ways of looking.

In the car scene: instead of developing an analysis of the underlying needs — which came from my questions, not hers — I could have started from what she had said. The exhaustion of these dinners. The tiredness of watching the same dynamics play out without anyone naming them. And from there, find something shared: we were both looking at something that wasn't working. We weren't looking at it from the same place. But we were looking at the same thing.

That shift — starting from the other rather than from yourself — doesn't require abandoning your own way of seeing. It requires not making it the starting point of every exchange.

### Bridge 3 — Name the Distance Without Making It a Problem to Solve

This is perhaps the most counterintuitive bridge. It consists of making the divergence itself visible — not to resolve it, but so it stops being an unspoken weight that bears down on every conversation.

Something like: "I realize we don't always come to this subject from the same place. You tend to look at what's concretely happening for people. I often look for the dynamic underneath. Both are true — but it means we don't always find each other."

This kind of statement does several things at once. It names the difference without ranking it — neither reading is presented as the correct one. It gives the other person a way to understand what is happening in conversations that go in circles — not as a failure, but as a difference in perspective. And it opens the possibility of talking about it — or not, at the other person's own pace.

It's not a guarantee. The other person may not be ready to hear it, or may not know what to do with it. But naming the distance, at least once, with the right words, changes something in the way it is inhabited. It moves from a floating, unspoken problem to a reality that both people have at least seen together.

## What These Bridges Don't Do

They don't synchronize two developmental trajectories that have diverged. They don't make difficult conversations suddenly fluid. They don't close a gap that has widened over years in a few well-formulated exchanges.

What they do is make the distance livable. They create spaces of genuine connection within a system that lacked them — not by erasing the difference, but by stopping it from contaminating every exchange.

And sometimes, that's enough for something new to become possible. Not the imagined relationship — a real one, built from what is actually there, not from what you wish were there.

That's a significant difference. And it is, perhaps, the most honest way of loving someone who doesn't look at the world from the same place as you.
