
**The clumsy art of hurting those we love**

You take her in your arms, in front of friends, and say: "My little wife."

The words slip out on their own, as they always do. She is 5'9" (1m75), you are 6'2" (1m88)—the height difference makes the expression seem almost logical. No one at the table thinks twice about it. You yourself don't notice a thing. It’s just a phrase you’ve been using for years.

She, however, feels something tighten, for a fraction of a second, behind the smile she flashes back at you. She is tall, and rather proud of it—it’s part of who she is, her presence, the space she occupies in a room. And now, the man who is supposed to truly see her labels her with the exact opposite word. _Little._ Not malicious. Just slightly off-target.

She says nothing. She laughs, a bit, along with the table.

Later, it’s her turn. She pulls out her phone for a photo, asks you to turn around, and says, almost by reflex: "Suck in your stomach." She’s only thinking about the photo—she wants a nice, sharp, flattering memory. But what your body hears isn't a framing instruction. It hears: right now, looking like that, you are too fat.

You suck in your stomach. You smile for the photo.

Neither of you meant to hurt each other that night. "My little wife" even carries a bit of pride in having her on your arm. "Suck in your stomach" is just about the lighting. If someone had asked you, leaving the dinner, "did you hurt each other?", you would have laughed at the question.

That is precisely what makes this kind of scene so hard to see coming: it is dressed up as kindness. We don’t tease to cause pain—we tease thinking we are creating a bond, a sense of lightness. And that sincerity is exactly what prevents us from looking at what the word actually does when repeated for the hundredth time.

Because it is never a word chosen at random. "Little" touches a place where she doesn't recognize herself. "Stomach" touches a place where you are already uncomfortable. We don’t just pick any random detail—without knowing it, we look for the one that causes a slight flinch, just enough to keep things light, never enough to cause a scene. It’s a fine dosage. And a dosage implies that we know precisely where it stings a little—which is hardly surprising, since this is the person we know best in the world.

So why that word, and not another? For you, "my little wife" was never a way to diminish her—it’s a clumsy way of saying _I know you, we have our own language._ For her, "suck in your stomach" might stem from an insecurity that isn't yours, but hers, regarding the image, redirected as an instruction rather than an admission. And there is also something more social, which no one would willingly admit: in front of a dinner table, the word that gets a laugh establishes a position, the one controlling the moment—easier than making oneself the butt of the joke.

You could have stopped there, two separate clumsinesses with no sequel. But these phrases kept coming back, month after month, until they became a kind of signature—spoken with the same smile each time, like two lines from a play you know by heart.

Why replay this tiny harm to each other night after night, instead of simply stopping?

Eric Berne had an answer to this question, decades before people started talking about everyday emotional abuse in couples. For him, every human being has a vital need to be recognized by another—a _stroke_, he called it, a relational touch, the sign that someone truly sees us. And his most unsettling discovery was that a total absence of strokes often feels heavier than a negative stroke. That’s why we keep going: "my little wife," repeated despite the harm it does, remains a point of contact. A clumsy way to keep touching each other, rather than risking silence.

But here is where it gets interesting, and where we must watch out for a false assumption: it doesn't mean we have to prick each other to stay alive. We live in an era that cultivates a need for intense sensations in everything—we have to feel, "live life to the fullest," be constantly stimulated, as if an absence of intensity meant an absence of life. The negative stroke easily fits into this mindset: at least there is juice, substance, something happening. But it is not the only alternative to silence. Between the jab that hurts and the silence that dares not speak—the silence of a couple too polite to say what’s wrong for fear of hurting—there is a third position: being fully present to oneself, without needing the other to prick you to feel like you exist, and being capable of naming a disagreement directly, without detour or dosage.

It is this third way that remains to be explored—how to exist for one another without depending on negative strokes, and without keeping quiet about what is wrong.

For us, it took time to see it. Not for a lack of self-reflection—we know how to face our difficulties, we’ve been practicing for years. But this one had slipped into our blind spot, disguised as tenderness, a private joke, a habit we no longer questioned. We had to name it once and for all. We talked about it, truly—not to justify ourselves, but to look at what it did to us, to both of us. And it was unpleasant to hear at the time. We saw precisely what it touched, each on our own side.

Since then, we’ve stopped teasing each other about those things. And above all, we’ve learned to ask for connection more directly, without taking a detour through sharp humor. Now, it can be something as simple as: "I need to connect, can we take five minutes to talk?" Or just a hug. It’s no more complicated than that—it’s just more exposed. We say what we want, without disguising it as a joke that would do the work for us.
