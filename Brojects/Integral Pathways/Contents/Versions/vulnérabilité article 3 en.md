### What You Think You Read in Him Might Not Be What’s Happening

On Friday evening, I told my partner I needed to spend the weekend alone.

Not because something was wrong between us. Not because I was exhausted by her, or her son. Simply because I had a mental space to construct—articles to write, a vision to map out, a project that demanded silence and internal availability. The kind of work you can't do in fragments, tucked between two moments of family life.

It was a simple need. Clear. Unambiguous in my mind.

What happened next was anything but.

Within hours, her reading of my request had produced a complete narrative: I couldn't stand them anymore. Her son was too heavy a burden. I was looking for an exit. We could never truly build something together. And to compensate for what she believed she had detected, she shifted into something strange—a form of excessive, almost maternal care, as if coddling me would fix what she imagined was broken.

I watched it all unfold with a mix of tenderness and perplexity.

Because what she read into my withdrawal had nothing to do with what was actually happening.

---

### The problem isn't the withdrawal. It's the reading we make of it.

In the previous article, we explored "phantom reciprocity"—that configuration where one person structurally carries and the other is carried, without the roles ever being able to reverse.

What fuels this configuration, often, is not a lack of love. Nor is it the fear of intimacy, as often as that is invoked. It is something more precise, and more correctable: **the automatic interpretation of the other's internal states.**

When he withdraws, an intention is attributed to him. A meaning. A story about what it says about him, about oneself, about the relationship.

_He shuts down—therefore he’s afraid. He needs space—therefore I’m suffocating him. He is silent—therefore something is wrong between us._

These readings are fast, often unconscious, and they share one common trait: they transform a **state** into a **message**. And in doing so, they trigger a response—pursuit, compensation, anxiety, reciprocal withdrawal—that has nothing to do with what the other person was truly experiencing.

This is where many relational cycles ignite. Not based on what actually happened, but on the interpretation of what happened.

---

### Why we interpret rather than read

The short answer: because interpretation is faster, and because it gives the illusion of control.

Naming what the other person feels—even falsely—reduces uncertainty. And uncertainty, in an attachment context, is one of the most uncomfortable experiences there is. The brain prefers a bad explanation to no explanation at all.

John Bowlby already observed this: the attachment system is a threat-detection system. When the bond seems threatened—when the other person withdraws, goes quiet, asks for space—the system activates, seeks an explanation, and clings to the first one that lowers the alarm.

The problem is that this explanation is almost always constructed from **one's own fears**, not from the **other's actual state.**

My partner didn't read my need for space. She read her fear of being abandoned. And she projected it onto my behavior with a precision that seemed obvious—until we talked about it.

---

### What true reading would require

Reading the other’s state—without interpreting it—is a skill. It is not a natural disposition; it’s not something we do instinctively when the attachment system is activated.

It requires one specific thing: **suspending the narrative** you are building about what the other's behavior means, and returning to what is observable.

Not "he’s withdrawing because he’s afraid," but: "he is withdrawing. What do I actually know about what is happening inside him right now?"

Most of the time, the honest answer is: not much. And that is uncomfortable. Because "not much" leaves the uncertainty wide open.

But it is precisely in this space of uncertainty—the one we avoid by interpreting—that true connection becomes possible. Because we can then ask a real question, instead of responding to an imaginary story.

### What male withdrawal really hides—three states everyone confuses

When a man withdraws, there are three distinct states that common interpretation lumps under a single label: "he’s afraid of intimacy" or "he doesn't want to commit."

These three states do not require the same response. Confusing them is almost a guarantee of making the situation worse.

**State 1—Saturation**

The system is full. Not of _you_, not of the relationship—of _everything_. Too many demands, too much accumulated cognitive or emotional load, too little space to reconstitute. Withdrawal here is a physiological regulation, not a relational message.

- **What it signals:** I need emptiness to become available again.
    
- **What it does NOT signal:** I want to leave, you’re suffocating me, something is wrong between us.
    

**State 2—Defensive Activation**

Something in the interaction has triggered a protective response. Not necessarily an attack—sometimes an intonation, a phrasing, a perceived pressure. Withdrawal here is a distancing from the trigger, not the person.

- **What it signals:** Something put me in protection mode; I need the pressure to drop to regain access to myself.
    
- **What it does NOT signal:** I don’t love you anymore, you said something terrible.
    

**State 3—The Fade-out (Effacement)**

This is the least visible and most misunderstood state. The man is not saturated, not defensive—he is absent to himself. Lost in rumination, a vague preoccupation, a temporary loss of meaning. The withdrawal here isn't directed outward—it is turned inward, toward something that often has nothing to do with the relationship.

- **What it signals:** I am not "there," even for myself.
    
- **What it does NOT signal:** You have no place in my life.
    

Responding to saturation as if it were defensive activation—by insisting, asking questions, or trying to "solve" it—aggravates both.

### The Three-Step Protocol: Read Before Responding

This protocol does not require the other person to explain themselves. It is practiced alone, in real-time, in the seconds following the perception of withdrawal.

**Step 1—Name the rising interpretation**

Before doing anything, identify the automatic narrative being built. Not to judge it—but to see it. _"I am telling myself that..."_ Say it out loud if possible, or write it mentally. This step alone creates distance between you and the reaction that narrative was about to trigger.

- _Ex: "I am telling myself he doesn't want to be here anymore." / "I am telling myself I’m suffocating him."_
    

**Step 2—Return to the observable**

Ask yourself: _what do I actually see?_ Not what it means. What is observable. A behavior. An expression. Timing.

- _"He has been silent since we got home." / "He answered in monosyllables at dinner." / "He went straight to the other room."_
    
    Stay there. Do not cross the line into interpretation yet. The observable is all you know with certainty.
    

**Step 3—Ask an open question, without charge**

Not "Are you mad at me?"—which already presupposes an answer and creates pressure. Not "What's wrong?"—which assumes something _is_ wrong and often triggers defensive activation.

Use an open question, without presupposition, with a neutral or soft tone:

- _"You seem deep in your head—do you need anything?"_
    
- _"I feel you're absent, are we good?"_
    
- _"Do you want to talk, or do you need your space right now?"_
    

These formulations signal your presence without creating pressure, and they give him the opportunity to name his state himself—which is infinitely more effective than attributing it to him from the outside.

---

### What this protocol does NOT do

It does not resolve deep attachment dynamics. It does not replace inner work. It does not guarantee the other will respond or be able to name what is happening.

**What it does:** It interrupts the automatic cycle: _interpretation → inappropriate reaction → aggravation_. It creates a few seconds of space between the stimulus and the response. And in that space, something different becomes possible.

It is modest. And it is already a lot.
