### Growing up without a witness — the cost of the other not seeing who you’ve become

_Series "Growing up — and not losing the other" · Article 6 · Subscriber exclusive_

That evening, after the phone call with her parents, I sat down next to her. I asked her if she was okay. She said yes; well, you know how it is.

I described this scene in the first article of this series. I described it from the perspective of what I saw—the mechanism, the role, the game being replayed. From my reading of what was happening on her end.

What I didn’t describe was what was happening on my end during that moment. Not the analysis—but what I was experiencing, internally, while I chose to remain silent.

There was something I now recognized as a specific kind of loneliness. Not the loneliness of isolation—we were sitting right next to each other, our shoulders almost touching. It was a loneliness of a different nature. The loneliness of carrying something real, of having truly walked through it, and being unable to share it with the person closest to you. Not because they refused, but because they didn’t yet have access to that place.

I was carrying a transformation that no one in my immediate life truly saw.

#### What it means to grow up without a witness

There is something that personal development doesn't prepare you for. It prepares you for the work—therapy, training, questioning oneself. It prepares you with tools—how to manage a conflict, how to express a need, how to break out of a role. It sometimes prepares the relationship—how to communicate better, how to create more connection.

It does not prepare you for what it feels like to undergo a real transformation without someone there to witness it.

A witness is not someone who understands everything you have been through. It is not someone who shares the same references, the same tools, or the same vocabulary. It is simply someone who sees—without needing to understand it all—that something has changed. That who you are today is not exactly who you were before. That this journey happened, and that it mattered.

When that witness is absent—and in a relationship where the asymmetry of growth is real, it is often absent—something happens. Not immediately. Gradually, silently, over time.

You begin to exist in two parallel spaces. The shared space—daily life, projects, ordinary conversations, the real love that continues despite everything. And an internal space that has no translation within the relationship. One that exists, that is real, but that is never truly named between the two of you.

And that space, by virtue of not being named, gains weight.

#### The forms this void takes

This lack does not always manifest clearly. It doesn't necessarily look like sadness or anger. It takes on more diffuse forms, harder to identify.

Sometimes it is a slight weariness in conversations. Not because you no longer love the other person, but because you know in advance that certain subjects will lead nowhere. An anticipation of the limit before you’ve even tried.

Sometimes it is a tendency to look elsewhere for what the relationship cannot provide. Not necessarily another person—a group, a community of practice, people who share the same vocabulary, the same references, the same relationship to inner work. These spaces gradually become more nourishing than some of the spaces shared with one's partner. This is not a betrayal. It is an adaptation.

Sometimes it is a form of diffuse resentment—difficult to justify, difficult to articulate. Not against something the other person did. Against something they don't do, don't see, or cannot yet see. A resentment against an absence rather than an act.

And sometimes, it is simpler and more painful than all that: it is the fatigue of not being seen. Of carrying something real, of having truly walked through it, and continuing to inhabit it alone.

#### What this void does to the relationship

There is a mechanism within this void that deserves to be named clearly—because it produces exactly the opposite of what we want.

When the need to be seen is not named for what it is—a need for real, legitimate recognition, which does not require the other to understand everything we’ve been through, just to see that it existed—it shifts. It transforms into an implicit expectation. Into a hope that the other will spontaneously notice, without being told, what has changed. Into a slight disappointment when they don't notice. Into a distance that takes root.

The "missionary" from Article 4, viewed from this vantage point, is no longer just someone who wants to transmit knowledge. It is someone who is trying to be seen—and who has found, without knowing it, a way of asking that cannot produce what they are looking for. Because we don't feel recognized when someone understands our analysis. We feel recognized when someone sees what it cost us to build it.

These are two very different things. And confusing the two maintains an expectation that the relationship cannot satisfy—not because it is flawed, but because we are asking it for something it does not have the means to give in its current form.

#### What belongs to the couple—and what doesn't

The first thing to do with this void is to sort it out. Not to minimize it—but to avoid making the relationship carry the weight of the whole thing.

Because part of this need to be seen does indeed belong to the couple. The partner can, under certain conditions, in certain moments, be more of a witness. Not by understanding everything—but by seeing. By saying, simply: "I’m not sure I understand everything you’re going through, but I see that it’s real and that it matters to you." That sentence, short and without the pretense of grasping it all, is sometimes all we need.

But another part of this need does not belong to the relationship. And making it carry that part is unfair—and ineffective.

The need to be seen in one's transformation by someone who truly understands it—who has been through similar things, who shares the same vocabulary, who can measure the path traveled—this need requires spaces other than the couple. Friends who share the same approach. Peer groups. A therapist, a supervisor, a community of practice. People who can be witnesses to the exact way this need demands to be satisfied.

Confusing the two—expecting from your partner what only these other spaces can provide—is one of the most common ways to put a relationship under pressure it cannot sustain.

Concretely, it looks like a simple question to ask yourself, honestly: Does the other person currently have the means to give me what I am waiting for—given where they are, what they know, and what they have been through? If the answer is no, the next question is: where is the right space for this need?

#### Three ways to create conditions to be more "seen"

What follows is not a guarantee. It is what changed something for me—in how I carried this void, and in what it produced in our relationship.

**1. Name the need directly—without dressing it up as transmission**

The difference between "I want to tell you about what I've been through" and "I need you to see what this has been like for me" is enormous. The first formulation initiates a transmission—it asks for attention, interest, perhaps understanding. The second formulation names a need for recognition—it asks for something much simpler and much more intimate.

The other person may not be able to respond to the first. They can often respond to the second. Because it doesn't ask them to understand—it asks them to see. And to see someone you love, even without understanding everything they are going through, is something most people can do when asked clearly.

I learned to say, in certain moments: "I don't need you to understand everything I’ve been through. I just need you to know that it was real, and that it mattered." That sentence, the first time I truly said it, produced something that hours of explanation never had.

**2. Build external spaces for what doesn't belong to the relationship**

This sounds like a retreat. In reality, it is a way of respecting the relationship—by not asking it to be what it cannot be.

Concretely: identify the needs for recognition that require an "initiated" witness—someone who understands the vocabulary, who has been through similar things, who can measure the journey. And seek those witnesses out deliberately, elsewhere. Not to replace the relationship—but to relieve it of an expectation it cannot satisfy.

This shift changes something unexpected. When the need to be seen is partially satisfied elsewhere, the implicit expectation toward the partner diminishes. And when expectation diminishes, something in the relationship breathes differently.

**3. Receive what the other can give—even if it’s not what you wanted**

This is perhaps the most difficult shift. Because it requires letting go of something—the image of what recognition should look like—to receive what is actually offered.

She might not see the mechanism I identified. She might not understand why a particular realization was a turning point. But she can see that I have been different for the past few months. That I react differently to certain situations. That something in my way of being present has changed.

That kind of recognition—without vocabulary, without a theoretical framework, from her own way of seeing—is true recognition. It doesn't look like what we would have wanted. It is real.

Learning to receive it for what it is—not devaluing it because it doesn't come with the words we would have chosen—is a form of self-work that personal development rarely mentions. Receiving what is given to us, in the form in which it is given.

#### What this series has traversed

We started with a car, on a Sunday evening, and a silence that weighed more than it seemed. We looked at what deforms when one grows and the other does not—the roles, the transactions, the ways of reading the world. We named the trap of the missionary, the trap of explanation, the confusion between understanding and transforming. We traversed the question of the return—how to remain oneself without it becoming an additional distance.

And we arrive here, at what is perhaps the most intimate knot of this entire journey. Not what growth does to the relationship—but what it does to the one who is growing. What they carry, alone, in the spaces that the relationship does not yet have the means to inhabit.

It is not a question that gets resolved. It is a question that is lived through—with more or less lucidity, more or less lightness, depending on the day and the moment.

What changes, when you begin to see it for what it is, is the way you carry it. No longer as a debt the relationship owes you. But as a real part of what it means to love someone who is not yet looking at the world from the same place you are.

And holding that—truly holding it, without erasing it, without making it a grievance, without waiting for it to change—is perhaps, at heart, one of the most honest forms of love.
