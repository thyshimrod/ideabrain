# What Your Growth Does to Your Roles — Without You Noticing

_"Growing — Without Losing Each Other" Series · Article 2 · For subscribers_

---

That evening, after the phone call with her parents, I sat down next to her. I asked if she was okay. She said yes, well, you know how it is. I said yes.

What I didn't say — what I could see clearly, from where I stood — was what had just happened in that call. The way she had stepped outside herself. That old, automatic posture, the posture of the child who agrees just to make it stop. I could see the mechanism. I could see what it produced in her, in us, in the hours that would follow. And I said nothing.

What I couldn't yet see — not that evening — was what my silence was doing, too, to our relationship. And what my gaze, even silent, even caring, was quietly installing between us without my having chosen it.

## What Distorts Without Being Chosen

When you do serious inner work — therapy, training, reading, practice — something changes in the way you are in relationship. You become more attentive. You read situations more clearly. You notice mechanisms that most people don't see. You develop a capacity for observation and analysis that, in a consulting room or a training space, is a valuable skill.

In a couple, that same skill can produce something unexpected.

Not because it's bad. Because it creates an asymmetry. An asymmetry of reading — one person sees what the other doesn't see yet — which translates, without anyone deciding it, into a relational asymmetry. And that asymmetry wakes up roles. Old, familiar roles, installed long before the inner work began. Or new, subtler ones, introduced by personal development itself.

That drift is hard to see. Because from the inside, it looks like care. Like clarity. Like love, even.

## The Three Faces of the Role That Reinstalls Itself

There are three configurations I encounter regularly — in my own experience and in that of the couples I work with. They don't exclude each other. They can coexist, follow one another, overlap.

### The Knower

This is the hardest role to see because it doesn't always show up in words. It shows up in a look. A slight hesitation before answering. A "yes" that arrives too quickly and means something else. A silence that says more than a reply.

The Knower sees. He can read situations, identify mechanisms, name what's happening. And that ability, even held in silence, even never spoken, creates something in the relationship. The other senses it — not always consciously, but they sense it. They sense they are being read. Observed. Analyzed, perhaps. And faced with that gaze, even a caring one, something closes.

This isn't paranoia. It's a normal response to a real asymmetry.

The Knower isn't trying to dominate. He is often trying to protect — not to hurt, not to impose his vision. But the protection itself becomes a form of elevation. I know, and I choose not to tell you. That choice, however generous, maintains a distance.

### The Involuntary Normative Parent

This one tends to arrive with the best intentions. You've learned NVC — you rephrase, you observe without judging, you distinguish facts from interpretations. You've worked on wounds — you notice disproportionate reactions, you can name emotional states. You've integrated TA — you identify crossed transactions, psychological games, life positions.

And without realizing it, you begin to correct. Not openly — subtly. You rephrase what the other said "to make it clearer." You ask questions that steer toward an insight. You gently signal that such-and-such a reaction "might come from" something older.

These are well-intentioned interventions. And they carry, despite everything, an implicit evaluation. A standard. A way of saying, without saying it: what you're doing there could be done differently. Better. With more awareness.

The other doesn't feel helped. They feel corrected. And the difference between the two — even when the words are gentle — is immediate to feel.

### The Savior — Personal Development Edition

This is the most refined version of the Karpman triangle. The classic Savior is easy to spot — he steps in unsolicited, offers solutions, takes charge. The personal development Savior is quieter.

He creates space. He asks open questions. He "accompanies" — without forcing, without imposing, with full respect for the processes he has learned. Except that this accompaniment wasn't asked for. The other didn't request a therapeutic space inside their relationship. They just wanted to talk, or not talk, or simply exist without being guided toward something.

The personal development Savior creates an invisible debt. A debt of consciousness — the other owes him, implicitly, for having been seen, supported, oriented. And that debt weighs, even when no one names it. It installs a quiet hierarchy: the one who accompanies and the one who is accompanied. The one who knows where to go and the one who is guided.

This is no longer a couple. It's a therapeutic relationship that no one chose.

## What These Roles Have in Common — and Why They Resist

These three configurations share a common structure. In each one, the person who has grown occupies, without deciding to, a high position. Not a position of contempt — a position of reading. They see more, understand more, can name what the other cannot yet name. And that position, even held with tenderness, even never claimed, creates an imbalance in the relational system.

What makes these roles particularly resistant is that they are reinforced by personal development itself. The more you work on yourself, the more you see. The more you see, the wider the asymmetry grows. And the wider the asymmetry grows, the more the roles solidify — because they give structure to something that would otherwise be too uncomfortable to hold.

There is also something deeper. These roles protect. The Knower who stays silent protects the other from a truth they can't yet hear — and protects himself from the rejection that truth might provoke. The Involuntary Normative Parent maintains a sense of competence and usefulness in a relationship where he no longer always knows how to exist otherwise. The personal development Savior fills a need for connection — even if the connection he creates is asymmetrical.

Seeing these functions doesn't erase them. But it changes the relationship one has with them.

## The Protocol — Recalibrating Without Confronting

What follows is not a method for "correcting" the other, or for erasing the roles. It's work on your own position in the system — which is, in any case, the only place where real action is possible.

### Step 1 — The solo inventory: identifying which role you're speaking from

Before acting, there is observation work to do alone. Not a self-flagellation session — an honest mapping.

Look back over the last few weeks. Recall three or four exchange moments with your partner — ordinary moments, not necessarily charged ones. And for each, ask yourself:

Did I hold something back that I knew, to protect? _(Knower)_ Did I rephrase, orient, or "help" without being asked? _(Normative Parent)_ Did I create an accompanying space in a moment that didn't call for it? _(Savior)_

The goal isn't to find a clean answer. It's to begin seeing the pattern — which configuration appears most often, in which situations it gets triggered, what it's trying to protect.

This work takes time. It deserves to be done in writing — not in your head, where rationalizations arrive too quickly. One page, a few days of observation, is enough to start seeing something real.

### Step 2 — Recalibrating your position: coming down from the elevated view without regressing

Once the role is identified, the temptation is to do the opposite — to stay even more silent, to step back, to "give space." That isn't recalibration. It's a role shift — from Knower to something passive that creates a different kind of imbalance.

Recalibrating doesn't mean erasing what you know. It means changing the position from which you speak.

Concretely, it looks like this:

Instead of rephrasing what the other said to "clarify" it — share what you yourself felt in the situation. Not what you analyzed. What you experienced. _"When it goes like this, I feel..."_ rather than _"What's happening here is..."_

Instead of asking a question that steers toward an insight — ask a question that expresses genuine curiosity about the other's experience. Not _"Don't you think this comes from..."_ but _"How did you experience it?"_

Instead of creating an accompanying space — be present without an agenda. Sitting there, available, without waiting for something to happen. Without your presence carrying a therapeutic intention.

This shift is small. It doesn't resolve everything. But it changes something in the texture of the relationship — because it puts both people on the same level, even temporarily.

### Step 3 — Opening up together, if the other is available

This third step is only possible if one condition is met: the other is in a space where a conversation about the relationship itself is possible. Not coming out of a difficult call with her parents. Not in the middle of a full day. In a moment of relative lightness, of connection already present.

This isn't a confrontation. It isn't a feedback session. It's an opening — short, honest, spoken from the "I."

Something like: _"I've realized that since I've been so deeply involved in this inner work, I sometimes feel like I'm observing more than I'm truly being with you. I don't know if you feel that too. But I wanted to name it."_

No expected response. No obligatory follow-up. The opening does its work — or doesn't, at the other's own pace.

What matters in this formulation is what it doesn't do. It doesn't analyze the other. It doesn't name what the other does or doesn't do. It speaks about yourself — about what you observed in your own way of being present. That is the only position from which this conversation can be held without triggering defensiveness.

## What This Changes — and What It Doesn't

This protocol doesn't resolve the asymmetry. It doesn't make the difference in reading, vocabulary, and references disappear. It doesn't magically synchronize two developmental trajectories that have diverged.

What it does is put movement back into a system that had grown rigid around roles. It creates a slight opening — in the way you position yourself, in the way you speak, in the way you are present. And that opening, if held over time, changes something in what the other perceives of you.

Not because you erased what you know. Because you chose to stop making it the center of gravity of the relationship.

That is a fine distinction. And it is, perhaps, the most important one this series will explore.
