
# You solved a problem that didn't exist.

## That's why she said never mind.

  

She hangs up. The call with her mother spiraled once again — a remark about her upbringing, about what she should have done differently, about what she is still doing wrong today. She comes to you and tells you about it. And you do what you do best: you think. You find an angle. You should tell her it's none of your business anymore. _You could space out the calls. Next time, cut it short before it spirals._

She listens to you for two minutes. Then she says: never mind.

And you don't understand. You just gave her exactly what she needed to fix the problem.

Except there was no problem to fix. Not the one you think.

----

What she brought to you wasn't a question. It was pain. And you heard a question.

That's no coincidence. Faced with the pain of someone you love, there is something unbearable about being able to do nothing. So you do something — you look for a solution, you propose a plan, you activate what you do best. Not for her, in reality. For you. So you don't have to stand there, helpless, in front of a suffering you cannot fix.

That's the 'Yes, but' game. You suggest. She objects. You suggest again. She objects again. And the more you insist, the lonelier she feels — not with a problem, but with a pain that no one seems to want to just acknowledge.

---

Did you notice that you never asked her what she wanted?

  

Not out of neglect. Because asking implies accepting that you don't know. And you know. You always have something to suggest. It's probably even what people appreciate about you in other contexts — your ability to find solutions, to move forward, to not get stuck.

---

But here, that is not what is being asked of you. And as long as you don't see it, you will keep offering answers to a question she never asked.

On her end, what happens when she says never mind?

She isn't giving up on being heard. She is giving up on you as the person capable of hearing her in this exact moment. 'Never mind' isn't closing the conversation. It's a realization —_ you don't see what's happening, and continuing to explain myself to you costs me more than it helps._

And you are left with the feeling of having been useless. Worse: rejected for trying to help. That's what hurts — not the fact that she's upset, but the fact that your sincere intention served no purpose.

---

Here is what is never said in this kind of scene: there is no bad intention anywhere. You truly wanted to help her. She truly wanted to be heard. And yet, both of you end up frustrated.

Not because one of you is wrong. Because no one named what was actually being asked.

Have you ever asked her, before proposing anything: do you want us to look for a solution, or do you just need me to listen?

That single line changes everything. Not because it's magic. Because it moves you out of the Savior role — the one who has to fix things — toward something simpler and more uncomfortable: presence, with nothing to do.

---

What that requires isn't technical skill. It's tolerating being useless for a moment, and staying anyway.

Because the truth is, what she expects from you regarding her mother probably isn't a strategy. It's not being alone with that pain. And no solution can replace that.

The question isn't how to give better advice. It's: are you capable of sitting there, fixing nothing, just so she isn't alone?
