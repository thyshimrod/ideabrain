**"You've done the work. You're still in the same scene."**

---

You know what a psychological game is. You can probably name the triangle — Rescuer, Persecutor, Victim. You've read the books, done some version of therapy or coaching, and developed a real capacity to see what's happening in your relationship.

And yet.

Last week, or last night, or this morning — you ended up in the same scene again. Same dynamic, different trigger. You said something you've said before, or didn't say something you should have, or felt that familiar closing-off that you recognize but can't seem to stop.

This isn't a failure of intelligence. It's not even a failure of effort. It's something more specific — and more interesting.

---

Understanding a mechanism and seeing yourself inside it are two different things.

You can watch a current from the riverbank and still get swept away when you step in. The current doesn't care that you can describe it. It responds to where your foot lands, not to what you know.

Relational games work the same way. The moment you enter the game isn't a moment of ignorance — it's a moment of automatism. Something happens, something shifts, and before you've had time to think, you're already playing your role. Not because you forgot what you know. Because knowing happens in your head, and the game happens somewhere faster than that.

The people most caught by this are often the ones who've done the most work. Because they've developed something the game knows how to use: a sophisticated way of being right about what's happening while still being inside it.

---

There's a particular version of this I see often.

The partner who is genuinely trying. Who listens, who reflects, who brings real effort to the relationship. Who can identify, usually correctly, what the other person is doing that isn't helping. Who has, somewhere in the background, a quiet but persistent sense that they are the one making the effort — and a confusion about why that doesn't seem to change anything.

That person is usually playing a role too. Not a dramatic one. A subtle one — the kind that feels like virtue, like presence, like love. The kind that's hardest to see precisely because it looks like the right thing.

That's the angle most self-help misses. It's easier to see the game when you're clearly the victim, or clearly the one creating damage. It's much harder when your role is the one that feels like care.
