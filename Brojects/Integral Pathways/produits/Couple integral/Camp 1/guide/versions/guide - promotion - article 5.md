**"You don't exit the game by understanding it better."**

---

If you've read the last four essays, something has probably happened.

Not a revelation. Something more modest and more useful: a slight shift in how you see yourself in the dynamic. A recognition that landed somewhere specific — not "I do that sometimes" but "that's actually how it works for me." A phrase that stayed with you longer than you expected.

Or maybe nothing landed cleanly, and you're still not sure which role is yours. That's information too.

Either way, this is where most relational work stops. You understand something now that you didn't understand before, or understand more precisely. And the implicit promise of that understanding is that things will be different — that knowing will translate, somehow, into changing.

It usually doesn't. Not by itself.

---

Here's what actually happens.

You have a moment of recognition — real, specific, uncomfortable in the right way. You see yourself in the Rescuer, or the Persecutor, or the Victim. You understand, maybe for the first time with this kind of precision, what the role is costing you and what it's protecting you from.

And then that evening, or the next day, or sometime that week — the scene happens again. The same signal, the same automatic response, the same role. Not because you forgot what you understood. Because understanding happens in your head, and the game happens somewhere faster than that.

This isn't a failure of intelligence or effort. It's the nature of what we're dealing with. The role you play preferentially is the one that feels most natural — most justified, most like the obvious response to what's happening. It doesn't feel like a role. It feels like reality.

Which means that understanding it, even clearly, doesn't make it feel less real in the moment. It just gives you something to analyze afterward.

---

What actually creates change is something smaller and more specific.

Not a better understanding of the pattern. Not more insight into where it comes from or what it's protecting. Those things matter — but they happen at the level of comprehension. What changes behavior is something that happens earlier: catching the moment you enter the game, before the role has fully taken hold.

That moment is brief. Often physical. Always personal. A particular quality of tension, or urgency, or closing-off — something that precedes the thought, precedes the reaction, precedes the scene that's about to unfold. It happens in a fraction of a second. And in that fraction of a second, something different is still possible.

One second is enough. Not to resolve the scene. Not to have a different conversation. Just to pause the automatic and introduce a choice — even a small one, even an imperfect one. That pause, practiced over time, is what actually changes the pattern.

But you can't practice catching a signal you haven't yet identified as yours.
