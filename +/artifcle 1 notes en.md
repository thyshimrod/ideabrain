## D2 — The invisible cost

There's something the Rescuer never really says.

Not the evening's reproach. Not the sigh when picking things back up. The real conversation — the one where you say: I'm exhausted from holding everything together, and I need you to take your share.

Because the other person doesn't see the list. They see someone who manages well. Who knows what they're doing. Who just prefers it that way.

And meanwhile, the list keeps growing.

---

## D3 — The Wooden Leg

"I don't know which program to use."

Maybe that's true. Maybe not. But this incompetence has a function: it exonerates. It avoids the mistake, the evaluation, the criticism that it wasn't done the right way.

In Transactional Analysis, this is called the Wooden Leg. And what's interesting isn't whether it's conscious or not. It's asking yourself: who benefits from things staying exactly as they are?

---

## D4 — The switch

The Rescuer doesn't become a Persecutor because they're a bad person.

They become a Persecutor because they've been carrying too much for too long without anyone seeing it. The evening's reproach isn't an attack. It's an overflow. On the wrong subject, at the wrong moment — but for a very specific reason.

The argument is about laundry. It's about something else entirely.

---

## D5 — What control is hiding

Picking the laundry back up is also picking control back up.

And control has its benefits. As long as you do everything, you're right. You're indispensable. Nothing can be done wrong.

But have you ever considered that this control costs you more than it protects you?

---

## D6 — Both sides of the game

What makes this scene hard to see is that both people have something to gain from it.

The Rescuer keeps control and is always right. The Wooden Leg avoids mistakes and doesn't have to carry the mental load.

Two roles. Two hidden benefits. And a scene that's been running for years without anyone really asking the question: do I actually want to keep playing this role?

---

## D7 — The real question

We spend a lot of time negotiating tasks. Setting up systems. Telling ourselves that this time, things will change.

And six months later, the scene starts again — with a different backdrop.

The real question isn't how to better divide the chores. It's: what have you been pretending not to see all along?
